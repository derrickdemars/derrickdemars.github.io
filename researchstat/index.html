Research Statement

Derrick DeMars, Ph.D. · Graph Theorist and Mathematics Educator
I. Overview

Classical Ramsey theory studies the patterns that cannot be avoided: color the edges of a large enough complete graph, and some monochromatic structure must appear. Anti-Ramsey theory asks the opposite question: how many colors can be used before a rainbow copy of a subgraph, one whose edges all receive different colors, is forced? The two theories pull in opposite directions. Forbidding monochromatic copies of a graph F pushes a coloring toward many colors, while forbidding rainbow copies of a graph H pushes it toward few.

My research studies what happens when both constraints act at once. The central object is the mixed Ramsey spectrum MRS(Kn; F, H), the set of integers k for which some edge-coloring of Kn with exactly k colors contains no monochromatic F and no rainbow H. Rather than asking what a coloring must contain, the mixed question asks which color counts are possible. The answer is not a single threshold but a set, and the colorings that survive both constraints turn out to be far more rigid than either constraint alone predicts. That rigidity is the subject of my work.

A recurring organizing tool is the Gallai coloring, an edge-coloring of a complete graph with no rainbow triangle. Gallai’s theorem gives such colorings a recursive block structure that is constrained yet flexible, which makes them natural scaffolds for mixed Ramsey constructions. Across my work, Gallai structure serves not only as a restriction but as an engine for building colorings with a prescribed number of colors.

II. Dissertation Results (Papers 1–3)

My dissertation at Auburn University, written under Peter Johnson, determined mixed Ramsey spectra for small forbidden graphs and for families of cycles. This work earned a Departmental Citation in 2022 for exceptional contributions.

Triangles (Paper 1). With Johnson, I studied colorings of Kn in which every triangle uses exactly two colors, so that no triangle is monochromatic or rainbow. We showed that MRS(Kn; K3, K3) = {g(n), …, n − 1}, where g(n) ∈ {⌈2 log5 n⌉, ⌈2 log5 n⌉ + 1}, using a result of Chung and Graham and an inductive argument. The result set a pattern for later work: isolate a structural barrier, then build colorings that meet it with equality.

Odd cycles and cycles (Paper 2). Also with Johnson, I extended the mixed spectrum to families of graphs and determined it completely for forbidding monochromatic odd cycles and rainbow cycles: MRS(Kn; {odd cycles}, {cycles}) = {⌈log2 n⌉, …, n − 1} for all n ≥ 2. The lower end comes from a balanced binary-tree construction on Gallai partitions that forces every color class to be bipartite while excluding rainbow cycles, and merging colors level by level fills in every value in between. As a corollary, grk({odd cycles} : {cycles}) ≤ 2k + 1.

Four-cycles (Paper 3). Building on the work of Axenovich and Choi, I gave an elementary, self-contained proof that max MRS(Kn; C4, C4) = n for all n ≥ 4. The argument isolates reusable lemmas on rainbow subgraph growth and chord-forcing on cycles. For general cycle lengths, a join-of-cliques layering yields the contiguous block {n + 2 − m, …, n + 1 − m + (m − 12)} ⊆ MRS(Kn; Cm, Cm) for all 3 ≤ m ≤ n.

III. Recent Work (Papers 4–6)

Since the dissertation, my work has moved from computing spectra toward explaining the rigidity behind them.

Bipartite collapse (Paper 4). Within any Gallai coloring, forbidding the two shortest monochromatic odd cycles, the triangle and the five-cycle, forces every color class to be bipartite, recovering the classical bipartite-cover threshold. The paper determines exact values and classifies the five extremal colorings at three colors, a distinction finer than any monochromatic degree sequence can detect.

Exact coincidence with anti-Ramsey numbers (Paper 5). I show that forbidding a monochromatic subgraph alongside a rainbow cycle costs no colors: for the cycle family treated, the top of the mixed spectrum equals the anti-Ramsey number. This sharpens a 2010 theorem of Axenovich and Choi, which established the agreement only up to an additive error, to an exact result. The proof uses a Hoffman block coloring, an elementary edge-cut lemma, and the anti-Ramsey values of Montellano-Ballesteros and Neumann-Lara.

Five-cycles (Paper 6). This paper initiates the study of colorings with no monochromatic and no rainbow five-cycle, a setting where no Gallai-type structure theorem is available. Using a substitution framework, a seven-vertex Gallai block, and a binary-tree covering argument, I prove a rate-two ceiling and the local rigidity of the Bondy–Erdős doubling. The common thread across these papers is rigidity: at the ends of the spectrum, the colorings that survive are far more constrained than either condition alone would suggest.

V. Methods

My arguments combine extremal and stability methods with structural decompositions, chiefly Gallai partitions and substitution, and with explicit constructions of my own design: balanced binary trees of Gallai blocks, join-of-cliques layerings, and Hoffman block colorings. Upper bounds typically come from structural lemmas that locate a forced substructure; lower bounds come from constructions that meet those lemmas with equality.

When a question reduces to a finite case, I encode it as a Boolean satisfiability problem and settle it with a modern CDCL solver, primarily Kissat. This lets me verify small cases exactly, find extremal colorings that suggest general constructions, and rule out candidate configurations with certainty. I also use AI-assisted exploration to generate and prune candidate structures before committing to a proof strategy. These tools do not replace proof; they shorten the path to the right conjecture and the right construction.

VI. Future Directions

Further cycle families. The spectra for triangles, four-cycles, and all odd cycles are now understood, and five-cycles are underway. I will extend exact spectra to longer cycle pairs (Cm, Cm), where parity matters: even cycles are harder to exclude as rainbow subgraphs, because the bipartite Gallai scaffolds that block monochromatic odd cycles do nothing to block even ones. I will also interpolate between forbidding all odd cycles and forbidding a single odd cycle, measuring how the bottom of the spectrum moves as the forbidden family shrinks.

Sparser host graphs. Nearly all mixed Ramsey results take the host graph to be complete. I will study mixed spectra on sparser hosts, for example bipartite and multipartite graphs, and ask which complete-graph phenomena, including Gallai-type rigidity, survive when many edges are missing.

When mixed and anti-Ramsey coincide. Because the anti-Ramsey number of H caps the number of colors in any coloring with no rainbow H, it always bounds the top of the spectrum. Paper 5 shows that for a family of cycles the bound is attained exactly. The natural next step is a general account: for which pairs (F, H) does max MRS(Kn; F, H) equal the anti-Ramsey number, and what structure appears when it does not? A characterization of equality would let the extensive anti-Ramsey literature transfer directly to mixed problems.

VII. Outlook

Taken together, these results treat mixed Ramsey spectra, anti-Ramsey thresholds, and Gallai–Ramsey numbers as different views of one phenomenon: the rigidity that appears when monochromatic and rainbow constraints act together. The program moves from exact finite spectra, through the structure of extremal colorings, toward general principles for when the mixed and anti-Ramsey pictures agree. It fits naturally with research groups in extremal and structural graph theory, and it offers entry points at every level, from small cases well suited to computation and undergraduate research to structural questions that call for new methods. My aim is to turn a collection of case studies into a coherent theory, with exact theorems, explicit constructions, and tools that others can build on.
