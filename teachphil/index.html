Teaching Philosophy

Derrick DeMars, Ph.D. · Graph Theorist and Mathematics Educator
I. Why I Teach

I teach mathematics so that students learn to think with structure: to identify patterns, evaluate conjectures, and articulate the reasoning behind an argument, rather than execute procedures by rote. That conviction was shaped in two complementary settings. At Auburn University, where I earned my Ph.D., mentors pressed me to make structure visible and to communicate it clearly, and I learned the precision of a well-posed question and the elegance of a tight proof. At Innova Preparatory School, I learned how much structure depends on people: routines that lower anxiety, choices that build agency, and feedback that turns struggle into momentum. Rigor becomes reachable only when a course is designed for belonging and persistence.

II. Inside the Classroom

A typical class opens with a question that is simple to state and hard to dismiss. In a discrete mathematics unit, I might put two edge-colored graphs on the board and ask: what is the real difference between them? Students name what they see, and I resist defining terms too early. We test intuitions against counterexamples and keep a visible record of what survives. Only then do we crystallize definitions and lemmas, so that definitions feel earned rather than imposed, and proofs become a way to make a discovered structure permanent. This mirrors my research in mixed Ramsey theory, where constructions and obstructions decide what is possible. Socratic questions drive the discussion (Why does this counterexample matter? What would have to be true for our claim to hold?), and transparent criteria, spaced retrieval, and chances to revise turn those habits into durable skill.

III. Designing for Every Learner

At Innova, a small non-public school serving students with dyslexia, ADHD, and autism, I teach grades 6 through 12, from middle school mathematics through Algebra I–III, Geometry, and AP Precalculus, in classes capped at ten. I read and implement IEPs and design curricula aligned to the Mississippi College- and Career-Readiness Standards. That work taught me to treat pedagogy as design: predictable routines, multisensory explanations, memory aids that reduce cognitive load, and accommodations that remove irrelevant barriers without lowering the standard. I differentiate deliberately, closing gaps for some students while extending others, because one pace for all is neither equitable nor intellectually honest. My students have posted consistent benchmark growth every year, and I was named Teacher of the Year in 2024–2025 and again in 2025–2026.

IV. Course Design and Assessment

Assessment is where I make rigor visible and inclusion non-negotiable. I begin with a small set of measurable outcomes, such as “students can write and critique proofs by induction” or “students can explain why a counterexample invalidates a claim,” then align daily activities, homework, and exams to them so students never have to guess what counts as understanding. After each assessment, I analyze performance by outcome: I reteach where misconceptions cluster, add retrieval practice for fragile skills, and extend for students ready to generalize. It is the same loop I use now to build pacing guides and lessons.

V. Mentoring

I take mentoring to mean structure plus autonomy. In Fall 2024, I mentored a student teacher through planning, co-teaching, and evidence-based debriefs: what students did, what they said, and what that implies for the next lesson. That rhythm of plan, try, analyze, and adjust also anchored my own growth at Auburn. At a research university, I would bring the same stance to undergraduate researchers and graduate students, with clear milestones, frequent feedback, and growing ownership, along with reading groups that demystify proof-writing and workshops that make unwritten expectations explicit.

VI. Belonging in Mathematics

Growing up in Côte d’Ivoire and Senegal taught me early that fluency is not a single switch: a student can be highly capable while still decoding unfamiliar idioms, pacing, and cultural references. So I treat language load not as a deficit but as an access variable I can design around. I normalize multiple “Englishes” in discussion, teach discussion norms explicitly, and build participation structures such as think-pair-share and small groups, so that mathematical discourse is learned rather than assumed. I also lean on multiple representations (symbolic, graphical, and verbal), because students often understand an idea before they can name it. These choices matter doubly for first-generation and international students, who may arrive with real talent but uneven preparation for the hidden curriculum of proof-writing.

VII. Teaching at a Research University

I am prepared to teach undergraduate service courses and proof-based courses aligned with my research. My discrete mathematics and graph theory modules use problem sequences that reveal structure: students explore counterexamples and extremal cases first, then formalize the patterns into lemmas. For graduate students, I bring a research practice in mixed Ramsey theory and Gallai structure, recognized with a Departmental Citation from Auburn, and a habit of showing how proofs are built, communicated, and extended. Above all, I keep iterating, using classroom evidence and student voice to improve each course. I teach so that students learn how mathematical ideas are built, not just what we already know. When they can spot structure, explain it, and extend it, they become independent learners, and that is the through-line from my research to my teaching.
